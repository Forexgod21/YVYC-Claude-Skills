# Advocacy Campaign Doctrine

**Attribution:** YourVisionYourCreation LLC — yourvisionyourcreation.com
**Doctrine class:** YVYC original — nonprofit category

---

## Universal So-What

"Raising awareness" is where advocacy goes to feel productive while
changing nothing. Awareness is ammunition, not a target. A campaign
is defined by three things or it is not a campaign: a specific
decision, a specific person or body with the power to make it, and a
specific ask they can say yes to. Everything else — the stories, the
data, the events, the coalition — exists to move that yes.

---

## Core Doctrine

### 1. The Decision Target — Define It or Decline It

Before any campaign activity:

- **The decision:** the specific, concrete change sought — a policy
  adopted, a budget line funded, a rule amended, a program continued.
  "Better support for veterans" is a value; "the county funds two
  peer support specialist positions in the FY27 budget" is a target.
- **The decider:** the named person or body that can actually grant
  it — and the verification that they CAN. Campaigns aimed at people
  without authority over the ask are theater with logistics.
- **The ask:** phrased as something the decider can say YES to,
  within their power, on a real timeline (budget cycles, legislative
  sessions, board calendars — the ask arrives when the decision is
  live, not when the campaign feels ready)

### 2. The Power Map

The decider does not decide in a vacuum. Map who moves them:

- **Influencers:** whose counsel does the decider seek — staff,
  colleagues, donors, constituencies they answer to?
- **Allies / persuadables / opposition:** placed honestly on the map.
  Campaign energy goes to PERSUADABLES — preaching to allies feels
  good and moves nothing; converting the committed opposition is a
  budget with no ceiling.
- **The messenger rule:** the most effective messenger is the one
  the decider cannot dismiss — constituents outrank outsiders,
  affected people outrank advocates, unexpected allies (the business
  owner, the sheriff, the conservative veteran for a progressive
  ask or the reverse) outrank predictable ones

### 3. Story-Data Interlock

Neither carries a campaign alone:

- The STORY makes the problem human and un-look-away-able: one
  affected person, specific, consented, dignified
- The DATA makes the problem un-dismissable as an anecdote: scale,
  cost, trend — local numbers for local deciders
- The interlock: story opens the door, data holds it open, the ask
  walks through. In testimony, in meetings, in media — same
  sequence.
- **Consent doctrine is absolute:** every story used is consented in
  writing, with the storyteller controlling their level of
  identification, informed of where the story travels, and holding
  the right to withdraw. Advocacy that spends vulnerable people's
  stories without governance is extraction wearing a cause.

### 4. The Escalation Ladder

Tactics are rungs, climbed in order, each rung earned:

1. The private meeting and the direct ask
2. The public record: comment periods, testimony, letters
3. Coalition voice: organizational sign-ons, joint letters
4. Media and public pressure
5. Sustained public campaign

- Start at the lowest rung that can win — escalation costs
  relationship capital that de-escalation never fully refunds
- Every rung documents the ask and the response: the record of
  reasonable asks reasonably refused is what legitimizes the next
  rung
- Burned bridges are budgeted deliberately or not at all: today's
  opponent chairs tomorrow's committee

### 5. Coalition Discipline

- The shared goal is written and signed before joint action —
  coalitions fracture at the first tactical disagreement when the
  strategic agreement was assumed
- Roles explicit: who speaks, who convenes, who holds relationships
  with which offices — freelancing in someone else's lane costs
  coalitions more than opposition does
- Credit is shared relentlessly; the cause outranks the logo
- The smallest-commitment tier is real: organizations that can only
  sign a letter are coalition members, not free riders — the ladder
  of engagement runs inside the coalition too

### 6. The Win Ledger and the Long Game

- Success metrics set at launch, at three levels: the decision
  (won/lost), the position gained (relationships, champions,
  record built), the capacity built (leaders developed, coalition
  infrastructure) — campaigns that "lose" the decision while
  building the other two often win the rematch
- The debrief is mandatory, win or lose: what moved the decider,
  what wasted effort, what the map got wrong — written while memory
  is fresh
- Wins are enforced: a policy adopted is not a policy implemented.
  The campaign's last phase is monitoring the yes until it is real.

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Busy campaign, nothing changes | Awareness with no decision target | Decision, decider, ask — or decline the campaign |
| Right message, wrong audience | Energy spent on allies | Power map; persuadables get the budget |
| Decider unmoved by outsiders | Messenger dismissible | Constituents and affected people carry the ask |
| Story backlash, trust destroyed | Consent treated as a formality | Written consent, storyteller control, right to withdraw |
| Scorched-earth opening move | Ladder skipped | Lowest rung that can win; escalation earned and documented |
| Coalition implodes mid-campaign | Strategic agreement assumed | Written shared goal and explicit roles before joint action |
| Policy won, never implemented | Campaign ended at the yes | Win enforcement is the final phase |

---

## Non-Negotiables

1. No campaign without a decision, a decider, and a yes-able ask.
2. The power map is drawn before tactics are chosen.
3. Story consent is written, controlled, and withdrawable — always.
4. Escalation is earned rung by rung and documented.
5. Coalition goals and roles are written before joint action.
6. The campaign ends when the yes is implemented, not when it is
   spoken.
